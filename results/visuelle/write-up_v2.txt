1. What Was Done

The forecasting experiments investigate whether Google Trends search interest
improves weekly sales forecasts beyond retail history, calendar variables, and
planned discounts. Weekly sales series were constructed for categories,
colours, and fabrics, with a stock-availability filter based on dated restock
records. The experiments forecast sales rates four and eight weeks ahead.

Forecast targets were split by calendar date, not by season label: training
weeks were before 26 November 2018, validation covered 26 November 2018 through
5 August 2019, and testing began on 12 August 2019. Models were fitted on
training rows only; historical observations before a forecast origin could
still be used across split boundaries for lagged features. Within each
horizon, model variants used the same usable rows and target weeks.

The comparison includes last-value and trailing-four-week-average baselines,
Ridge regression, XGBoost, and LightGBM. Both boosted-tree models were evaluated
with and without lagged search-interest features. Paired bootstrap confidence
intervals over test weeks were used to evaluate the effect of adding search.

2. What Was Found

Both boosted-tree models substantially outperformed the last-value baseline.
On the test set, XGBoost reduced MAE by approximately 36% at four weeks and
47% at eight weeks, while LightGBM reduced MAE by approximately 35% and 47%,
respectively. Without search, XGBoost achieved the lowest test WAPE at four
weeks (18.97%), while LightGBM achieved the lowest at eight weeks (21.39%).

Search impact is reported as WAPE with search minus WAPE without search, in
percentage points. A positive change means search worsened the forecast. For
LightGBM, search changed test WAPE by +0.22 percentage points at four weeks
(95% CI: -0.33 to +0.74) and -0.54 percentage points at eight weeks (95% CI:
-1.41 to +0.14). Both intervals include zero, so neither establishes a
reliable change.

For XGBoost, search changed WAPE by +1.05 percentage points at four weeks
(95% CI: +0.39 to +1.88) and +1.14 percentage points at eight weeks (95% CI:
-0.13 to +2.70). The four-week interval indicates a reliable deterioration in
this comparison; the eight-week interval does not establish a reliable change.
These results do not prove that search cannot help in other settings.

The no-search boosted-tree models are therefore the primary benchmarks:
XGBoost at four weeks and LightGBM at eight weeks, based on test WAPE.
Incomplete search coverage and alignment remain limitations. The stock filter
is also an imperfect proxy: some listing-weeks marked out of stock still have
recorded sales. Results describe recorded sales under this filter, not
unconstrained demand. Planned discounts are included as features, but their
individual contribution was not isolated and descriptive discount
associations should not be interpreted as causal.

3. What Changed from the Proposal, and Why?

The analysis was extended to include calendar-time weekly forecasting,
stock-availability filtering, four- and eight-week horizons, and comparisons
of boosted-tree models with and without lagged Google Trends features. Paired
weekly bootstrap intervals were introduced to assess search contribution.
These changes support a direct comparison of search features while reducing
the risk of interpreting stockout-related zero sales as weak demand.

The older search-contribution tables, explanation, figures, and figure
generator were removed because they reflected an earlier season-label split
and were not updated by the latest rerun. The current comparison uses the
calendar-date split and the results in scores.csv and predictions.csv.

The retained rolling_forecast_* files evaluate a separate binary-event
forecast: whether a product-retailer listing's cumulative sales over the next
four or eight weeks exceed zero. Their classification metrics are not
sales-volume or sales-rate forecast errors and do not support the comparisons
above. The original script was found in the separately supplied VISUELLE data
folder; it retrains every four weeks using labels ending before the origin.
All saved prediction labels matched the subsequent-sales definition when
checked against the supplied sales data. Origin-week feature availability
still needs verification, and the current script specifies XGBoost although
the saved predictions contain only LightGBM and logistic regression, so the
full saved run has not been reproduced.

The binary test outcomes were heavily imbalanced: 40,732 of 43,499 were
positive at four weeks (93.64%), and 20,467 of 20,475 were positive at eight
weeks (99.96%), leaving only eight negatives. An always-positive classifier
would achieve the same accuracy as the positive rate and 50% balanced
accuracy. Accuracy and precision alone are misleading in this setting, and
eight-week negative-class performance is especially fragile.

The files are retained as supplementary historical results, but excluded from
the project's trend-forecasting performance claims. Selling any units does not
mean a style is rising: this target does not measure growth direction, rank
emerging styles, predict sales rates, or isolate the contribution of search.
At four weeks, LightGBM's mean balanced accuracy across five seeds is 78.51%,
compared with 50% for the always-positive baseline, but this only assesses the
binary event. At eight weeks, its mean balanced accuracy is 56.97%; only eight
negative cases are available, so this result should not support a strong
conclusion.

Further work should improve stock reconstruction and search-data coverage and
alignment before drawing stronger conclusions about unconstrained demand or
the general value of search interest.
