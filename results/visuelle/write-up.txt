1. What I Did

I extended the existing forecasting experiments to investigate whether Google Trends search interest improves weekly sales forecasting beyond the information available from retail history, calendar variables, and planned discounts. I constructed calendar-time weekly sales series for categories, colours, and fabrics, and introduced a stock-availability filter using dated restock records. I then developed four- and eight-week-ahead forecasting experiments, comparing last-value and trailing-four-week-average baselines with Ridge regression and XGBoost, both with and without lagged search-interest features. Finally, I used paired bootstrap confidence intervals to evaluate whether adding search interest produced a reliable improvement in forecast accuracy.

2. What I Found

XGBoost achieved the strongest overall forecasting performance among the evaluated models, reducing test-set mean absolute error (MAE) by approximately 40% compared with the last-value baseline at the four-week horizon and 54% at the eight-week horizon. However, adding Google Trends did not produce a consistent, statistically reliable improvement in forecast accuracy. This suggests that retail-history and other forecasting features already captured much of the predictive information available in the current dataset.

The results support retaining XGBoost without search features as the primary benchmark for the new weekly forecasting experiments. Nevertheless, incomplete search-data coverage and imperfect stock reconstruction limit the conclusions that can be drawn. Further work should improve stock reconstruction and search-data coverage and alignment, and adopt a strictly chronological evaluation design to strengthen the reliability of the results.

3. What Changed from the Proposal, and Why?

Compared with the original proposal, I extended the analysis by introducing calendar-time weekly forecasting, stock-availability filtering, four- and eight-week forecast horizons, and a more direct comparison of models with and without lagged Google Trends features. I also used paired bootstrap confidence intervals to assess whether the contribution of search interest was statistically reliable.

These extensions were introduced to investigate the predictive value of search interest more directly, evaluate performance across different forecast horizons, and reduce the risk of interpreting stockout-related zero sales as low demand. The findings led to a more cautious conclusion than the initial expectation that search interest might improve forecasting: its additional predictive value was not demonstrated reliably under the current experimental conditions. The results also identified stock reconstruction, search-data coverage, and chronological evaluation as important areas for further improvement.
