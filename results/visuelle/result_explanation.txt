### Results explanation

The experiment investigated whether Google Trends search interest improves the forecasting of fashion-related sales beyond information available from retail sales history, calendar variables and planned discount conditions. The existing proposal established the data preparation and earlier experiments. The newly completed work extends this analysis by constructing calendar-time weekly series, incorporating a stock-availability filter, comparing models with and without lagged search interest, and evaluating genuine four- and eight-week-ahead forecasts.

The results show that XGBoost provides the strongest forecasting performance among the evaluated models. On the test set, its mean absolute error is approximately 40% lower than the last-value baseline at a four-week horizon and 54% lower at an eight-week horizon. However, adding Google Trends does not provide a consistent improvement. The paired bootstrap confidence intervals for the test-set MAE differences all include zero, indicating no reliable incremental benefit from search in these experiments. The same-week lag analysis reaches a similar conclusion: the best validation-selected lag does not produce a statistically reliable test-set WAPE improvement.

Partial search coverage, imperfect alignment between product-level search terms and aggregated sales categories, colours and fabrics, and the predictive information already contained in retail-history features may help explain why search adds little value. These findings do not establish that search is universally unhelpful; rather, they show that its contribution was not demonstrated under the current data coverage, aggregation and evaluation design.

Stock availability also affects interpretation. The experiment reconstructs stock from dated restock records and excludes listing-weeks estimated to have no stock at the beginning of the week. This reduces the risk of treating stockout-related zero sales as low demand. Nevertheless, some listing-weeks classified as out of stock still contain recorded sales, showing that reconstructed stock is an imperfect proxy. Consequently, the evaluation measures recorded sales under the specified stock-filtering procedure rather than unconstrained customer demand.

Discounts are included as forecasting features because observed sales vary across discount bands. Average monthly sales are highest in the 0–10% discount band and generally lower at deeper discount levels. This relationship is descriptive rather than causal: deeper discounts may occur later in a product's life, when demand is already declining. The current search ablation does not independently quantify the contribution of discounts, so a dedicated feature-ablation experiment would be required to measure their incremental predictive value.

Overall, the newly completed experiments support retaining the no-search XGBoost model as the primary benchmark. The evidence suggests that retail-history and planned retail features are more useful than the tested search features under the current experimental conditions. Further work should prioritise verifying stock reconstruction, improving search coverage and alignment, and using a strictly chronological evaluation design before drawing stronger conclusions about future demand forecasting.
